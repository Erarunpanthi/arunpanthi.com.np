Diversion Headworks: Weir, Barrage, Difference and Components

PSC Subjective Note · Nepal Engineering Service (Civil), Second Paper · Lok Sewa Aayog

Diversion Headworks

Diversion Headwork

Diversion headwork: A diversion headwork is a structure constructed across a river to divert the river water into the off-taking canal, and to regulate the supplies entering the canal. The canal takes off from one of the banks of the river.

A diversion headwork may be of two types, depending on the structure built across the river:

  • Weir – a raised crest with or without a small gate arrangement.
  • Barrage – a weir with a series of large gates across the whole width of the river.

Key idea: The fundamental difference is how the crest level and the water level are controlled. In a weir the crest itself is raised and the water spills over it; in a barrage the crest is kept low (almost at river-bed level) and the water level is raised by closing gates. A barrage therefore gives positive control over the river flow, while a weir does not.

Difference between Weir and Barrage

Difference between Weir and Barrage

The comparison between a weir and a barrage is the most frequently asked short question under diversion headworks. The differences are given below.

Table 1: Weir vs Barrage
SN Weir Barrage
1. Cost It is cheaper because of simple construction. It is relatively costly due to complicated structure construction.
2. Crest level A weir has a high crest level. It is with a lower crest level.
3. Afflux It provides more afflux (higher rise of water level upstream). It provides less afflux.
4. Control over river water It does not have control over all the river water. It provides effective control over the entire river flow.
5. Silting It causes more silting on the up-stream bed. It provides less silting on the up-stream bed.
6. Bridge for transportation Bridge cannot be constructed over a weir for transportation purposes. A bridge for transportation purpose can be constructed over a barrage.
7. Gate / shutter operation Crest shutters are difficult to operate, i.e. more time and labour is required. Gates are convenient to operate.

Reason behind the differences: Because the crest of a weir is raised, it creates a high afflux and the approach velocity drops upstream, so silt is deposited over a long reach of the up-stream bed. A barrage keeps the crest low and regulates flow by gates, so the afflux is less, velocities can be maintained to keep silt moving, and the gate-controlled crest also carries a road bridge and is easier to operate.

Types and Components

Types of Weir and Barrage

Types of Weir

  • Vertical drop weir: the crest is a vertical wall with an upstream and downstream floor; the simplest and cheapest form.
  • Dry stone slope weir: the vertical drop is replaced by a sloping dry stone pitching, which is suitable for small heights and permeable foundations.
  • Concrete slope weir: the downstream face is sloped and the u/s and d/s cut-offs and floors are of concrete; suitable where the drop is moderate.
  • Parabolic weir: the downstream face follows a parabolic profile, giving a better hydraulic shape and reducing the length of the floor.

Types of Barrage

  • Conventional barrage with vertical gates supported on piers.
  • Barrage with flat gates / falling shutters used for smaller openings.
  • Barrage with radial (Tainter) gates on the piers, commonly used for large openings because of easier operation and smaller hoisting effort.
  • Barrage with drum gates, used where a long clear waterway is required.

Components of Diversion Headworks

The main components of a diversion headwork are given below:

  • Weir or barrage – the main structure across the river that raises the water level and diverts the flow.
  • Divide wall – a wall built between the weir and the canal head regulator to separate the still pond from the silt pocket and to prevent cross-currents.
  • Canal head regulator – regulates the supply entering the off-taking canal.
  • Silt excluder / silt pocket – a pocket in front of the canal head regulator in which silt settles, and scouring sluices which flush the deposited silt downstream.
  • Scouring sluices – openings near the river bed that pass the silt-laden water downstream and maintain the approach channel.
  • Fish ladder – a passage for upstream migration of fish.
  • River training works – guide banks, marginal bunds and spurs that keep the river aligned and protect the structure.
  • Upstream and downstream protection works – floors, cut-off walls and aprons that check seepage and scour.
Summary

Summary

Key Points to Remember

  • Weir: high crest, more afflux, cheaper, less control, more silting, no bridge, hard shutter operation.
  • Barrage: low crest, less afflux, costly, complete control of flow, less silting, road bridge possible, easy gate operation.
  • A barrage is essentially a weir with large gates across the whole width of the river.
  • Diversion headworks include the weir/barrage, divide wall, canal head regulator, silt excluder, scouring sluices, fish ladder and river training works.
  • Afflux is the rise of water level on the upstream side due to the construction of the headwork; a high afflux is responsible for more silting upstream.

  • A 5-mark question almost always asks to differentiate weir and barrage; write a 6–7 point table, not paragraphs.
  • Keep the pairs in matching order: crest level, afflux, control, silting, cost, bridge, gate operation.
  • Finish with one sentence explaining that the differences follow from the crest level and the use of gates.

Define Weir and Barrage (5)

Weir: A weir is a masonry or concrete structure with a raised crest constructed across a river to raise the water level and divert the water into the off-taking canal. The water flows over the crest, and the crest may be fitted with small shutters to control the pond level.

Barrage: A barrage is a weir-like structure with a series of large gates across the entire width of the river, founded on a low crest that is almost at the river-bed level. The water level upstream is raised by closing the gates, which gives effective control over the entire river flow.

Why Does a Weir Cause More Silting than a Barrage? (5)

A weir has a high crest, which raises the pond level and therefore creates a large afflux. The rise in water level reduces the velocity of flow on the upstream side, and the reduced velocity is no longer able to carry the sediment load of the river, so silt is deposited over a long reach of the up-stream bed.

In a barrage, the crest is kept low and the pond level is controlled by gates. The afflux is therefore less, and the velocities can be maintained high enough to keep the silt in suspension and to flush it through the scouring sluices. Hence a barrage causes less silting upstream than a weir.

Explain the Components of Diversion Headworks (10)

  1. Weir or barrage: the main structure across the river that raises the water level and diverts water into the canal.
  2. Divide wall: separates the weir portion from the silt pocket and prevents cross-currents and eddies near the canal head regulator.
  3. Canal head regulator: regulates the discharge entering the canal and controls the silt entry.
  4. Silt excluder and silt pocket: the pocket allows silt to settle, and the excluder diverts the silt-laden bottom layers away from the canal.
  5. Scouring sluices: pass the deposited silt downstream and maintain the approach channel near the regulator.
  6. Fish ladder: provides a passage for fish to migrate upstream.
  7. River training works: guide banks, marginal bunds and spurs keep the river in its channel and protect the headwork.
  8. Protection works: upstream and downstream floors, cut-off walls and aprons check seepage, piping and scour.
